While the industry's attention stays on benchmark races, a quieter story is compounding: governments are becoming some of the largest operators of AI systems in the world.

## The workloads that matter

Public-sector AI rarely looks like a chatbot launch. It looks like:

- **Document processing at scale** — permits, tax filings, benefits applications, procurement reviews. Extraction and summarization workloads where a 30% error reduction is a measurable public good.
- **Translation and access** — services in dozens of languages that no agency could staff manually.
- **Fraud and anomaly detection** — unglamorous, effective, and politically safe when it works.
- **Citizen-facing assistants** — the highest-risk tier, where a hallucination becomes a headline.

## Why the model choice is different

Governments don't buy leaderboard rank; they buy procurement compliance, data residency, auditability, and price. That tilts the field in interesting directions — open-weight models with regional hosting, sovereign cloud deals, and flash-tier models that can be justified to an auditor.

<Callout type="signal" title="The sovereignty wedge">
Data-residency requirements are quietly the biggest friend open weights have. A model that can run inside a jurisdiction — on hardware the government controls — solves a compliance problem no API contract fully solves.
</Callout>

## The honest tension

Public deployment raises the stakes on failure modes the private sector tolerates: a bad answer about benefits eligibility isn't a UX bug, it's a denial of service to a person. The agencies doing this well are the ones treating the model as a component in a supervised workflow — not as an autonomous decision-maker.

Expect this channel to cover the wins and the failures with equal attention. The failures are usually more instructive.
